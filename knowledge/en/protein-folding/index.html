How does a long, thread-like chain of amino acids become a machine that does its own job?

The chain folds by itself, hiding from water and pulling on itself. The same sequence always folds into the same shape, and that shape decides what it binds and what it does. So it works only when it folds exactly.

What happened

  1. Chain: 20 kinds of beads in one lineThere are 20 kinds of amino acids, and the order in which they are joined is written in DNA. Right after joining, the chain is a line stretched out in one dimension. Each bead has its own nature: some like water, some dislike water (oil-like), and some carry a plus or minus charge.
  2. What folds the chainIn water, beads that dislike water clump together and hide inside, and beads that like water stay outside. Plus and minus pull on each other. The video’s “like magnets” is a metaphor for this pull; it is not a magnetic force. Of these, the force of hiding inside away from water (the hydrophobic interaction) is seen as the dominant one. The force between charges is probably not dominant, and hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces matter too (Dill et al. 2008).
  3. Same sequence, same shapeThe chain does not clump at random. The same sequence folds into the same shape every time. That is why there is a contest to guess the shape from the sequence alone (CASP, 1994), and AlphaFold predicts the structure from a sequence alone. An experiment in which an unfolded enzyme folded back by itself and regained its work showed this. Ribonuclease was turned into a randomly unfolded chain and then brought back, and its native disulfide bonds formed again almost completely. So the authors concluded that the information for the native structure is in the sequence (Anfinsen et al. 1961).
  4. The shape is the job: GFPDifferent shapes do different jobs. Only a partner that fits the set spot binds (a lock and key). GFP is a jellyfish fluorescent protein of 238 amino acids. Three of them make the light, but those three glow only when the whole chain folds exactly. The three are 65–67 in a row in the sequence (serine, tyrosine, glycine). They sit on the helix running through the middle of an 11-strand barrel. Only when folded into the native shape do the three close into a ring and oxidize into the part that gives light. This needs oxygen and takes about 4 hours at 22°C (Heim 1994, Ormö 1996, Yang 1996). The other 235 are the setting that holds the diamond.

How it differs from a snake cube

Alike
Pieces are strung on a cord. Each joint bends only a few ways. There are so many possibilities that trying them one by one never ends (10 to the 300th). When done, it becomes one shape.
Different
People solve the cube, but water and attraction solve the protein. The cube’s pieces are all the same, but there are 20 kinds of amino acids with different natures, and those differences choose the shape.

Common misconceptions

MisconceptionEvery protein has exactly one shape.

In factFor many proteins the folded shape decides the job, but many proteins also work unfolded, without a set shape. Such proteins often take their shape only when they bind a partner molecule (Wright and Dyson 1999). The “wobbly proteins” at the end of the video are these, and AlphaFold still does not predict them well.

Frequently asked questions

What is protein folding?

It is how a chain of amino acids joined in a line folds by itself in water into a set 3D shape. The same sequence folds into the same shape every time, and that shape decides what the protein binds and what it does.

What force folds a protein?

The force by which amino acids that dislike water gather inside, away from water (the hydrophobic interaction), is seen as the dominant one. Hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces matter too, and the force between charges is probably not what dominates folding (Dill et al. 2008).

Does the amino acid sequence alone decide a protein’s shape?

In 1961 Anfinsen and colleagues showed that when ribonuclease is turned into a randomly unfolded chain and brought back, its native disulfide bonds form again almost completely, and concluded that the information for the native structure is in the amino acid sequence. Many proteins, though, work without a set shape (Wright and Dyson 1999).

Why must GFP fold correctly to glow?

GFP is a jellyfish protein of 238 amino acids. The part that gives light forms when amino acids 65–67 close into a ring and oxidize, and the native folded shape is needed both for it to form and for it to glow. It needs oxygen and takes about 4 hours at 22°C (Heim et al. 1994, Ormö et al. 1996).
