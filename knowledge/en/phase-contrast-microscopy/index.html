How does a phase contrast microscope make transparent, invisible cells visible?

A phase contrast microscope uses two rings to send the light that passes straight through a cell and the light the cell spreads along different paths, then advances only the straight light by a quarter wavelength and dims it, so the two cancel in the image and the transparent cell looks dark.

In a brightfield microscope, colorless, transparent things such as bacteria and cells are hard to tell from the background. Brightfield forms its image from how much light the specimen absorbs, that is, from differences in color and brightness, and such specimens hardly absorb light; they only delay it a little (phase). Staining is often unsuitable for living specimens, and darkfield illumination easily leads to misreading fine structure. Closing the condenser aperture or defocusing brings a little contrast but blurs the detail. In the 1930s Frits Zernike devised a way to turn this delay into differences in brightness by placing a phase plate in the path of the light that goes straight through, and the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for this phase contrast method, especially for his invention of the phase contrast microscope.

01 Structure

Swap the condenser aperture for an annulus, and add a phase plate to the objective

Two places change from a brightfield microscope. The round iris of ⑤ the condenser becomes an annulus, and ⑧ the objective becomes a phase contrast objective with a phase plate at its back focal plane. Switch between the two with the buttons and the path of light and the camera image change together.

A phase plate (a thin ring of film) sits at that ring's place in the back focal plane and advances only S by a quarter wave while dimming it to 25%. The plate is not fitted separately; the Ph number on the barrel is the number of the annulus to use.

How it makes cells visible

  1. Split the light into two paths. ⑤ and the back focal plane of ⑧ are conjugate planes, so the ring of the annulus is imaged onto the ring of the phase plate. Light that goes straight through the specimen (S) gathers in this ring, and light spread by the cell (D) passes outside it.
  2. Advance and dim S alone. The ring of film on the phase plate advances only S by a quarter wave and cuts its intensity to 25%. D is left as it is.
  3. Cancel in the image. D already lags S by a quarter wave, so it is now half a wave out of step with the advanced S and the two cancel. The cell turns dark, and the camera only measures that brightness.

    Summary

    Two overlapping rings get past seeing brightness only

    1. Brightfield sees only brightness. A transparent cell only delays light, so in focus it is nearly as bright as the background.
    2. Add two rings. The condenser annulus and the phase plate at the objective's back focal plane sit in conjugate planes. The phase plate advances only the straight light by a quarter wave and dims it, so it cancels the spread light in the image and the cell looks dark.
    3. Keep the two rings on top of each other. Fit the annulus with the same number as the objective, open the condenser aperture fully, overlap the bright ring with the dark ring through a phase telescope, and look in focus. In wells, image at the center.

    MythA phase contrast image is computed by the camera or by software.

    In factThe phase plate (a glass plate) inside the objective and the interference of light make it. The camera is the same ordinary camera as in brightfield, and looking through the eyepieces gives the same. Computing phase-contrast-like images in software is a separate method.

    MythIn a phase contrast image, darker means thicker.

    In factHowever thick, the inside of a large object returns to the background brightness (shade-off), and a very large delay even makes an object brighter than the background.

    Questions

    Frequently asked questions

    What is a phase contrast microscope?

    An optical microscope that shows differences in how much a transparent specimen delays light (its phase) as differences in brightness. It is used to look at living, unstained cells. Frits Zernike devised it in the 1930s and received the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physics for it.

    How does phase contrast microscopy work?

    It takes two rings. The condenser annulus lets light in only through a ring, and the light that passes straight through the specimen (S) gathers on a ring of the same size on the phase plate inside the objective. The light spread by the cell (D) passes outside that ring. When the ring of film on the plate advances only S by a quarter wavelength and dims it, S ends up half a wave apart from D, which was already a quarter wave behind, and the two cancel in the image, so the cell looks dark.

    How does a phase contrast microscope differ in structure from a brightfield microscope?

    Two parts differ. Instead of the round iris at the condenser's front focal plane it uses an annulus with a ring-shaped opening, and it uses a phase contrast objective with a ring of film (the phase plate) at its back focal plane. The two rings sit on conjugate planes, so the image of the annulus lands on the ring of the phase plate. The light source, collector lens, field diaphragm, condenser, tube lens and camera are the same, and a green filter matched to the phase plate is recommended.

    What causes the halo and shade-off in phase contrast microscopy?

    The ring of the phase plate has a width, so part of the light spread by large, smooth structures passes through the ring with the straight light and is advanced and dimmed too. That light can no longer cancel, so the inside of a large object returns to the background brightness (shade-off) and a bright rim appears outside its edge (halo). Apodized phase plates, with neutral density rings beside the phase ring, reduce the halo.

    What do Ph1, Ph2 and Ph3 mean on a phase contrast microscope, and how do you match them?

    They are the codes of condenser annuli sized to match the phase rings of objectives. Usually PhL goes with 4× and 5×, Ph1 with 10× and 20×, Ph2 with 40× and 60×, and Ph3 with 100× objectives. Put in the annulus with the same code as the objective, lay the two rings on each other while watching through a phase telescope or Bertrand lens, and open the condenser aperture fully.

    Can you build a phase contrast microscope yourself?

    The phase plate is fixed inside a phase contrast objective, at its back focal plane, so you usually buy a phase contrast objective. The rest is an ordinary brightfield microscope. Place an annulus matched to the NA of that objective's phase ring at the condenser's front focal plane (radius = NA × condenser focal length), or, without a condenser, an LED ring above the specimen (Webb 2015), and match the size and the center while watching through a phase telescope. To use an objective without a phase ring, put the phase plate on a plane conjugate to the back focal plane formed by relay lenses (external phase contrast).

    Why does phase contrast work poorly in 96-well plates?

    Surface tension curves the medium in the well up toward the wall, so it bends the light like a lens and the image of the annulus slides off the ring of the phase plate (the meniscus effect). In one measurement with standard phase contrast, the area where phase contrast worked was 2.3% of the well in a 96-well plate (10×) and 25% in a 6-well plate (4×). Imaging at the center of the well and using larger wells or labware with a flat liquid surface helps.
